## 2. Smoke generation, aging, sampling and detector response

SAME's source was controlled overheating below ignition, not a generic flaming-fire soot source. Material, heating, flow and time in concentrated smoke all matter. The apparatus generated smoke, held it in an aging chamber, then delivered it to diagnostics. Consequently a “net aging” value, primary programmed hold, and an “aged 720 s” publication label are not automatically interchangeable. For example, the SAME-R CSV gives test 61 a net age of 38 s and 61a a net age of 807 s. `Rt/R0` is a resistance ratio; a temperature needs the applicable calibration. [SAME-2, PDF pp. 3–7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20130000422/downloads/20130000422.pdf)

The three moment instruments address different quantities: P-Trak particle number, a modified residential ion chamber's first-moment response, and material-calibrated DustTrak optical mass response. The particle count required dilution; DustTrak calibration involved aerosol material, optical properties and estimated density. The separately installed Shuttle and ISS detectors must not be conflated with that diagnostic ion chamber. The method defines count mean diameter as `M1/M0` and diameter of average mass as **`(M3/M0)^(1/3)`**. The latter's Table 1 heading omits the exponent; preserve the printed heading in provenance while using the explicit definition from PDF p. 3 in a typed quantity. Geometric mean and geometric standard deviation additionally assume a lognormal distribution. [SAME-2, PDF pp. 3–6](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20130000422/downloads/20130000422.pdf)

The workbook exposes the measurement chain directly: `Material Sort` **AV2** is ISS obscuration **in volts**, **AW2** ISS detector scatter in volts, **AX2** STS detector volts, **BD2/BE2/BK2** ion-B signal/offset/difference, **BR2** count mean diameter, **BS2** diameter of average mass, and **CB2–CD2** calibration factor, dilution factor and density. “Obscuration (volts)” is not percent obscuration. A detector voltage is not a universal particulate mass or an alarm event.

The excluded Kapton value is **AW50**, blank, flight 2, test 51a. This reproduces the descriptive counts 20/35 and 18/23. It does **not** establish 20 or 18 missed alarms, detector sensitivity, false-negative probability, or independent trial counts. Aging records, reused specimens, apparatus and calibration remain shared. A valid alarm claim needs the actual detector/channel transfer function, baseline, operating status, alarm logic and time record.

There is also a concrete unit trap: workbook `detection June 14` **H158/O158** sets a target **mass** alarm of **2 mg/m³**; it does not certify a 2 V cutoff. Its other target rows concern number concentration and ion moment, not a list of observed alarms. These cells and exclusions are preserved in `workbook_audit.json`.

The official 2018 NIST publication abstract reports that neither tested spacecraft detector detected every smoke type, while equivalent ground and flight smoke were not significantly different in that comparison. NIST's investigator account attributes frequent difficulty with Kapton/Teflon to the ISS light-scattering detector and easier response to cellulose, silicone and Pyrell. These are useful qualitative source findings, with the same study ancestry; they do not validate the particular 2 V row classification. [NIST publication, abstract](https://www.nist.gov/publications/evaluation-spacecraft-smoke-detector-performance-low-gravity-environment), [NIST investigator account, detector-results paragraphs](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2018/05/nistnasa-study-shows-one-detector-doesnt-fit-all-smoke-spacecraft)

## 3. Detection timing, toxic products, ventilation and cleanup

The earlier statement “981 s alarm delay” should be replaced by a **source-specific clock value**. The 2024 preliminary Saffire paper chooses a 1 mg/m³ reference and reports 981 s for VI-3. The 2026 paper chooses 2 mg/m³ total particulate measured by the FFD DustTrak DRX. Its Table 1 gives VI-3 as 1037 s. Different analysis thresholds explain why these are not interchangeable observations; they do not alone prove a 56 s improvement in a deployed detector. The 2026 text explains that most figures use an experiment timeline beginning about ten minutes before ignition, with Figure 5 specially shifted to igniter-off. The tabulated time origin still needs event-specific confirmation before presenting ignition-to-alarm latency, especially across PMMA preheat and thin-fuel runs. [2024, PDF p. 5 Fig. 7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240002981/downloads/Saffire-ICES-2024-ICES-365-accepted%20final.pdf), [2026, PDF pp. 5–9](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260001992/downloads/ICES%20Saffire%202026%2005%2004%20b.pdf)

The following is a visual transcription of **2026 Table 1, PDF p. 6**. Time is retained as printed. Gas values are **increases at the FFD**, not absolute cabin concentrations, exposure averages, or crew response deadlines.

| Flight/sample | Material and sides | Time to reference alarm, s | CO increase at alarm, ppm | CO₂ partial-pressure increase at alarm, mmHg |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| IV-2 | PMMA, 2 | 1083 | 63.2 | 1.28 |
| V-3 | PMMA, 1 | 1117 | 28.1 | 0.49 |
| VI-3 | PMMA, 2 | 1037 | 105 | 1.43 |
| VI-4 | PMMA, 1 | 1603 | 283 | 3.09 |

This supplies a useful positive answer: **at the same published aerosol reference level, the reported gas increments differed substantially among these seven events.** The authors compare some values with their cited 24-hour CO and 1-hour CO₂ limits. A momentary or maximum increment is not itself a reconstructed exposure over those durations. The capture does not provide complete pre-event baselines, exposure histories, or a new habitability assessment. Saffire's CO/CO₂ evidence also does not establish the absence of HCN or reactive acid gases.

Two additional provenance differences matter. The 2026 Table 1 caption says **opposed** flow, whereas 2024 Table 1 lists **concurrent** flow. Do not silently harmonize that field. The papers also report different maximum gas increments for the same event: VI-3 CO is 308 ppm in 2024 Table 2 and 338 ppm in 2026 Table 1; CO₂ increase is 3.51 versus 3.66 mmHg. Preserve separate analysis versions and their shared experiment ancestry. This report does not establish the reason for the revisions.

**Ventilation has multiple roles.** In SAME it changes source cooling, dilution and particle aging; in Saffire it also sets transport paths to the far-field instrument. The 2026 authors attribute relatively short smoke transport to a direct SFU–FFD path despite the instruments being at opposite ends of Cygnus, and caution that more extensively mixed geometries can differ. The measured FFD aerosol is therefore not a direct inventory of everything generated at the fire. The 2021 Saffire IV/V presentation reports submicron-dominated measured mass but also likely loss of larger particles at a clogged SFU outlet screen. [2026, PDF p. 7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260001992/downloads/ICES%20Saffire%202026%2005%2004%20b.pdf), [2021 slides, PDF p. 19 / slide 16](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210017343/downloads/ICES%202021%20Platform%20v2.pdf)

**Cleanup evidence is species- and event-specific.** The same presentation reports nominal CO₂ scrubbing but little CO removal by the smoke eater; catalyst, packing and sensor explanations remain hypotheses. Importantly, “no acid gas was observed” applies to the scheduled tracer-release events: PDF p. 17 / slide 14. The next page explicitly discusses **HCl observed during the later Saffire-IV burn-plus-release event**, with surface adsorption/saturation proposed as a possible explanation. Replace the broad “released acid gas was not seen” summary with this event distinction. A downstream nondetection does not certify source absence or successful cleanup. [2021 slides, PDF pp. 17–21 / slides 14–18](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210017343/downloads/ICES%202021%20Platform%20v2.pdf)

## 4. Suppression, cool flames and useful Earth relevance

FLEX separates **visible hot-flame loss**, later **cool-flame extinction**, droplet vaporization, disruption and end of observation. The detailed TP defines LLUV flame size by the outer OH* emission boundary and color-camera size by the visible boundary; a droplet leaving the camera field can end its trace without ending the test. It also records accumulated gas products between sequential tests and needle-contamination limitations. [FLEX TP, PDF pp. 14–15, 22–23 / printed pp. 8–9, 16–17](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150023456/downloads/20150023456.pdf)

The PSI FLEX CSV explicitly contains a **visible flame extinction diameter** and `Test end` labels, with no separate cool-flame endpoint column. Its `Burning rate; mm` header lacks the dimensional definition needed for comparison with a diameter-squared regression constant. Blank and en-dash entries remain missing/undefined. This table is not identical to the TP: it has 274 rows whereas the TP executive summary describes 284 tests; its first test reports initial diameter 1.69 mm, while TP Table I, PDF p. 16, prints 1.80 mm. The table includes helium cases not covered by the TP's reported nitrogen/CO₂ matrices. These are acquisition/version/measurement-definition questions, not permission to relabel 186 `Extinction` rows as complete extinguishment or infer that ten trials failed.

The 2014 primary cool-flame paper supplies the missing endpoint distinction: large n-heptane, n-octane and n-decane droplets could continue reacting after radiative loss of the hot flame, then undergo a separate extinction before vaporization. Its authors discuss different heat-transfer effects of CO₂ and helium and restrict a simple extinction-diameter comparison to like diluent conditions; that source does not support ranking agents for cabin fires. [Cool-flame extinction, PDF pp. 1–2, 7–8](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140006750/downloads/20140006750.pdf)

Water-mist evidence offers complementary positive results:

- **MIST STS-107:** increased water loading and smaller droplets suppressed the studied premixed propane-air flame propagation. This is a flame experiment with a stated geometry and mixture, not an extinguisher qualification. [MIST, PDF pp. 1, 3–4 / printed pp. 281, 283–284](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040053582/downloads/20040053582.pdf)
- **ISS FWM development:** the 2013 engineering units extinguished all 16 open-cabin, all 16 rack and all 16 elevated-oxygen test-standard fires; 15 of 16 battery tests succeeded, and the failing configuration was not selected. Battery performance concerned stopping thermal runaway, rather than simply timing visible flame loss. These are development-test results, not an in-orbit comparative agent trial or current operating instructions. [FWM development, PDF pp. 6–7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20130011664/downloads/20130011664.pdf)
- **Orion stored-energy work:** water spray and ISS fine water mist are distinct technologies. Laptop/case composition and propagation were consequential test variables. The paper describes toxic-product sampling but explicitly defers detailed toxicity results; it cannot supply missing species concentrations or prove water scrubbing effectiveness. [Orion PFE, PDF pp. 1–2, 11–12](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180005251/downloads/20180005251.pdf)

A precise Earth-relevance comparison is available from the NIST-hosted NRL/Colorado School of Mines methane/air study. Under its premixed atmospheric-pressure conditions, submicron mist reduced burning velocity more effectively by mass than water vapor. Its published model and measurements supported a small-drop limiting regime: once droplets evaporate in or just upstream of the flame, further size reduction need not improve inhibition at fixed loading. This qualifies the slogan “smaller is always better” while keeping gas-flame inhibition distinct from delivery through a real compartment. [Fuss et al., 2002, PDF pp. 1, 3, 6 / printed pp. 361, 363, 366](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/el/fire_research/Fuss-Inhibition-of-Premixed-Methane-Air-Flames-with-Water-Mist.pdf)

Engine low-temperature chemistry, nuisance-resistant smoke sensing and controlled mist delivery are credible research connections. They are not established commercial impacts of these particular flights. The historical NASA Spinoff examples remain separate technology-transfer histories, not outcomes attributed to SAME, FLEX or Saffire.
